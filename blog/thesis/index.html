When I was a PhD student, I wanted the students I worked with to leave knowing how to ask their own research questions, not just how to run someone else's experiments. That still shapes how I work with students and other collaborators. Some of these ideas come from Javier Rando's notes on supervising and running projects.

  • I like to start with a question the student already finds interesting. I can bring ideas of my own or help turn a broad interest into something we can test.
  • Learning a method, writing a strong thesis, and aiming for a paper can lead to different choices. It helps to know early which goal matters most to each of us.
  • A good experiment can change the question we thought we were asking. I take that as a sign that we learned something.
  • I want students to decide what to investigate next, not just implement my ideas. I can help think through the options when the next step is unclear.
  • The person doing the work often knows the details better than I do. I appreciate hearing when my advice doesn't fit and what they'd try instead.
  • Some of the best experiments start with an idea that isn't ready yet. Talking it through can make a small first test obvious.
  • Looking at individual successes and failures often tells us more about what to test next than looking at averages alone.
  • When someone is blocked on compute, a dataset, or an experiment that won't work, knowing what they've already tried can help us save days of work.
  • I'm happy to get questions as they come up rather than saving them for a meeting. Reminders are welcome if I owe someone something.
  • I'm happy to talk about careers or introduce students to other researchers when I can.
  • Different people need different kinds of support. I appreciate hearing when a different meeting schedule or a different way of getting feedback would help.
  • Quick questions usually work fine in writing. If there's nothing we need to talk through together, I'm happy to skip a meeting.
  • The best meetings start with a question we want to answer or a decision we want to make. A few examples are usually enough to get us started.
  • I don't need students to prove they were busy. I'd rather talk about what we learned, what's confusing, or what to try next.
