What being a PhD student taught me about collaboration

When I was a PhD student, I thought a lot about how to work with students on research projects. I wanted them to leave knowing how to ask their own research questions, not just how to run someone else's experiments. That still shapes how I like to work with students and other collaborators.

Some of these ideas are adapted from Javier Rando's notes on supervising and running projects.

Choose a question you want to answer

  • Start with what interests you. Tell me about a few things you keep coming back to. I can share ideas of my own or help turn a broad curiosity into a question we can actually test.
  • Agree on what you want out of the project. Learning a method, writing a strong thesis, and aiming for a paper can all lead to different choices. Say which matters most to you so we can plan around it.
  • Expect the question to change. An experiment might change how we frame the problem or send us toward a different question entirely. That's a normal result of learning something, not a sign that the project has failed.

Make the project your own

  • Lead the work. I want you to decide what to investigate next, not just implement my ideas. Feeling unsure about the next step is normal. We can talk through the options together.
  • Treat my suggestions as suggestions. You'll often know the details better than I do. Tell me when my advice doesn't fit and explain what you'd try instead.
  • Share ideas before they're polished. Some of the best experiments start with an idea you're not sure is worth bringing up. We can figure out what a small test would look like.
  • Look at individual results, not just averages. Read a few examples where a method worked and a few where it failed. The differences can tell us what to test next.

Ask early and keep each other informed

  • Ask when you're stuck. If you're missing compute, unsure which dataset to use, or frustrated by an experiment, tell me what you've tried and where you got stuck. We may be able to save you days of work.
  • Send questions when they come up. Don't wait for our next meeting. Remind me if I owe you something, too.
  • Ask about things beyond the project. If you want career advice or an introduction to another researcher, I'm happy to help where I can.
  • Tell me what's not working about how we work. Different people need different kinds of support. If you'd benefit from a different meeting rhythm or a different way of getting feedback, say so.

Use meetings to think together

  • Handle quick questions in writing. If there's nothing we need to discuss live, it's fine to cancel the meeting.
  • Bring a question or a decision. Start with what you want to leave the meeting knowing. If you're bringing results, pick a few examples we can look at together.
  • Don't feel you have to prove you were busy. I'd rather talk about what we learned, what's confusing, or what to try next than go through a list of completed tasks.
